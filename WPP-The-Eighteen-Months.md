## Prologue: What was at stake

To understand why the last eighteen months mattered so much, you have to remember what WPP was.

It began as Wire and Plastic Products, a maker of shopping baskets. Martin Sorrell took control in 1985 and spent three decades buying the best agencies in the world and bolting them together. By 2017 WPP was the largest advertising group on earth, worth around £24bn, and a fixture of the FTSE 100 for close to thirty years. It was one of a small handful of genuinely world-leading British companies — a crown jewel, and treated as one.

That is the altitude from which the fall should be measured. By late 2025 WPP was worth about £3.1bn. It had lost roughly seven-eighths of its peak value, and it was about to be removed from the index it had occupied for a generation.

This is the story of how that happened, what the board did about it, and why — as of this month — the company looks meaningfully different from the one that nearly fell over.

# Part One: What happened

## Chapter One — The warning
### April to July 2025

The decline did not arrive suddenly. It arrived as a series of downgrades, each one worse than the last.

WPP entered 2025 expecting a difficult year. Clients in consumer packaged goods — its largest sector at 28% of revenue — were cutting spend. So were technology clients. Neither was unusual on its own. What was unusual was the scale of what followed.

In the first half of 2025, two of the largest accounts in the industry walked. **Mars** moved its media business to Publicis. Then **Coca-Cola** moved its North American media business to Publicis as well. These were not small assignments at the margins of the business; they were among the most visible relationships in global advertising, and they left in the same window.

On **9 July 2025**, WPP cut its guidance. It told the market that revenue less pass-through costs would fall between 3% and 5% for the year, well below what it had previously signalled. The shares fell about 16% in a day, the worst performer in the FTSE 100.

The next morning — **10 July 2025**, a single day later — WPP announced its new chief executive.

That sequence tells you almost everything about the state of the company. A board does not announce a CEO transition twenty-four hours after a profit warning by coincidence. The decision had been made; the warning forced the timing.

Mark Read, who had led WPP since 2018 and steered it through the pandemic, would step down on 1 September. He stayed until the end of the year to hand over.

---

## Chapter Two — The choice
### July 2025

The obvious move would have been to hire an advertising veteran. Someone who had run a network, knew the pitch circuit, and could win back Coca-Cola.

The board did something else entirely.

**Cindy Rose** came from Microsoft, where she was Chief Operating Officer of the Global Enterprise business — the operation that helps the world's largest companies use technology and AI to change how they work. Before that she had been President of Microsoft Western Europe and, before that, CEO of Microsoft UK. Nine years at Microsoft in total. Earlier, she had run Vodafone's UK consumer business, held a senior role at Virgin Media, and spent fifteen years at Disney, finishing as Managing Director of Disney Interactive Media Group across EMEA.

What she had never done was run an advertising agency.

The trade press called her *"the CEO nobody saw coming."* Digiday called her an *"insider-outsider pick,"* and that phrase is the important one — because there was an inside. **Rose had sat on WPP's board as a non-executive director since 2019.** She had watched the decline from inside the boardroom for six years. She knew the business, the numbers and the people. She had simply never run them.

### What the board said, and what it meant

Chairman Philip Jansen was specific about the search and about the reasoning. The appointment, he said, followed *"a thorough selection process that considered both internal and external candidates,"* and as an existing board member *"she understands the business and the needs of clients."*

Then the part that reveals the strategy:

> *"Cindy has supported the digital transformation of large enterprises around the world — including embracing AI to create new customer experiences, business models and revenue streams."*

Read that again, because it is not a description of an advertising executive. It is a description of someone who changes how enterprises make money. **New business models. New revenue streams.** The board was not hiring someone to win back the media accounts. It was hiring someone to change what WPP sells and how it charges for it.

Jansen added that she had *"led multi-billion-dollar operations across the UK, EMEA and globally, built enduring client relationships and delivered growth in both enterprise and consumer environments."*

That last clause — **enterprise and consumer** — matters more than it looks. We return to it in Part Three.

### Her first words

Rose's debut message to staff set the tone: she was *"super-excited,"* and she would not *"sugar-coat"* the challenges.

At her first global town hall she named three principles for the new WPP: **put people first, win for clients, harness AI.** She said she wanted WPP to be *"the home for the world's most exceptional talent."* And she set an expectation that landed hard across a 100,000-person workforce: every employee would need to become an **AI super-user**.

She started on 1 September 2025.

## Chapter Three — The diagnosis
### September to October 2025

Sixty days in, the numbers got worse again.

On **30 October 2025**, WPP reported third-quarter revenue less pass-through costs of £2.459bn, down **5.9%** like-for-like. More damaging than the quarter itself was the guidance: the full-year shortfall was now expected at **5.5% to 6%**, downgraded from the 3% to 5% given in July. A second downgrade in four months.

Rose did not soften it. She called the performance **"unacceptable"** and said the company was *"taking action to address this."*

For a new chief executive sixty days into the job, inheriting a decline she had not caused, that word was a choice. It set a standard she would be measured against.

She then laid out a four-point plan:

1. **Simplify and integrate the client offer** around data and AI — simpler, more integrated, efficiently priced, designed to deliver business outcomes for clients.
2. **Lift execution** — strengthen go-to-market, dramatically simplify internal organisation, build a high-performance culture.
3. **Expand the addressable market** by pushing harder into enterprise and technology solutions.
4. **Strengthen the financial foundations** — cost efficiency, a strong balance sheet, disciplined capital allocation.

Full details, she said, would come *"early in the new year."*

And she gave the diagnosis a phrase that stuck. WPP, she said, needed to be *"a little less holdco and a little more 'co.'"*

### What she was actually saying

Underneath the four points was a single argument, and it is the argument the whole turnaround rests on.

WPP's problem was not that its people were not good enough. It was that the **structure** had become the obstacle. Decades of acquisitions had produced a holding company of competing agency brands, each with its own P&L, its own systems, its own leadership and its own incentive to protect its own revenue. A client wanting media, creative and production got three negotiations, three teams and three invoices.

Rose would later put the diagnosis plainly: *"our recent underperformance has been driven by excessive organisational complexity, a lack of an integrated operating model and inconsistent strategic execution."*

Not the market. Not AI. Not the clients. **Complexity.**

---

## Chapter Four — The platform
### October 2025

October 2025 was the month WPP's technology strategy became visible, and it happened in two moves two weeks apart.

**On 15 October**, WPP extended its partnership with Google for five years and committed **$400m** to Google technologies. The deal gave WPP early access to Google's newest models — Veo for video, Imagen for images — wired directly into WPP Open, WPP's AI marketing platform. The stated benefit was up to 70% faster production and double the asset utilisation.

**On 24 October**, WPP launched **Open Pro**.

This was the more significant of the two, and it was easy to miss. Open had existed for some time as internal infrastructure — the system WPP's own people used to plan, create and produce work. By this point it had **more than 75,000 internal users** and had been adopted by over **90% of client-facing staff**. Clients including Nestlé and Coca-Cola were using it.

Open Pro was different. It was a **self-serve version**, sold directly to marketers, priced on an access fee plus usage. It let a brand plan, create and publish campaigns — in WPP's own description — *without the help of an agency.*

An advertising group had just launched a product that lets you not hire an advertising group.

The man running it, **Matt McNeany**, had arrived from Omnicom in July 2025 as president of WPP Open. He described Open Pro as a way to reach businesses previously underserved by WPP — companies that could never afford a full-service integrated agency: startups, regional teams inside global brands, smaller businesses.

### What that actually meant

Three things were true at once, and holding all three is the key to the rest of this story.

**One.** Open was already working inside the business. 75,000 users is not a pilot.

**Two.** The published benefits were all expressed as **time saved**: fourteen hours a week returned to a four-person team; strategy and creative development compressed from four weeks to three hours; thirty-three times the asset volume on the Hawkstone Harvest IPA campaign, delivered by four people in two days.

**Three.** WPP bills most of its work by time.

Hold two and three together and you have the defining commercial problem of the whole AI transition, which we unpack in Part Two.

## Chapter Five — The bottom
### November to December 2025

Two things happened in late 2025 that marked the low point, and one that quietly marked the turn.

**On 26 November 2025**, Omnicom completed its acquisition of Interpublic. At a stroke, WPP's competitive position changed: Publicis had already passed it to become the world's largest advertising group, and now a merged Omnicom–IPG sat alongside. WPP went from first to third in roughly eighteen months.

**In December 2025**, WPP was removed from the FTSE 100 and dropped into the FTSE 250. Its shares had fallen around 62% across the year on the back of two profit warnings. After almost thirty years in the index, one of Britain's best-known companies was out.

The scale of the gap was stark. Across 2025, **Publicis grew organically by 5.5%. WPP fell 4.8%.** A ten-point spread between the leader and WPP in the same market, facing the same clients and the same AI disruption. Whatever was wrong was not the weather. It was WPP.

### And then, quietly, the turn

In the same month WPP left the index, **WPP Media won the UK Government's media account** — roughly **£1.5bn over four years**, taken from Omnicom's MG OMD. It was the largest single win of the period.

What matters is *why the government bought*. Its stated rationale was consolidation: reducing suppliers from **33 to 23**, cutting campaign planning times, lowering management costs, streamlining messaging across departments.

The UK Government did not buy creativity. It bought **simplicity**.

That is the first hard evidence that the thing Rose was doing internally was also something clients would pay for externally. The internal programme and the external proposition turned out to be the same argument.

## Chapter Six — The plan
### 26 February 2026

Rose unveiled **Elevate28** alongside the 2025 results, and the results gave her the mandate. Revenue less pass-through costs had fallen **5.4%** like-for-like to £10,176m — WPP's worst year since the pandemic. Headline operating margin had dropped from 15.0% to 13.0%.

*"You can see from our numbers this morning that our performance is not where it needs to be,"* she told journalists. And then the line that defines the strategy:

> *"What's become very clear is that what's made us successful in the past will not make us successful in the future."*

### What she committed to

**Structure.** WPP stops being a holding company and becomes **a single operating company**. Four divisions — Media, Creative, Production, Enterprise Solutions — across four regions: North America, Latin America, EMEA, APAC. Agency brands consolidate under WPP Creative.

**Cost.** **£500m** of gross annualised savings by 2028, at a restructuring cost of around £400m over two years.

**Timing.** Three phases. Stabilise new business and cut cost through 2026. Integrate go-to-market and return to organic growth **during 2027**. Emerge as a simpler, lower-cost, AI-enabled business growing faster **from 2028**.

She was blunt about the identity change: *"We don't want to be a holding company any more."* She framed Elevate28 as reaching *"the end of the job"* — a last reset rather than another in a series.

### The honest reading

This is a bet, and it should be described as one. WPP is dismantling the structure that made it the largest advertising company in the world, on the argument that the structure is now the thing holding it back. If Rose is right, WPP emerges as something closer to an integrated technology-and-services company. If she is wrong, it has spent £400m and three years dismantling its own distinctiveness.

The evidence since February suggests she is, so far, right.

---

## Chapter Seven — The turn
### March to August 2026

Here is where the story changes direction, and where most summaries of WPP still get it wrong — because the half-year numbers hide it.

**Quarter one was bad.** Revenue less pass-through costs fell **6.7%** like-for-like. Worse than the full-year 2025 figure. From the outside it looked like the decline was accelerating.

**Quarter two was not.** The same measure fell **2.8%**.

The rate of decline **more than halved in three months**. A 3.9 percentage point improvement in a single quarter.

The reported half-year figure was −4.7%, because that is what you get when you average a bad quarter and a much better one. Anyone reading only the half-year number concluded WPP was still falling at roughly the 2025 rate. Anyone reading the quarters saw a company that had stopped falling.

### What was driving it

**Clients stopped leaving.** Retention went from **16% to 43%** year on year — Huawei, Tesco and L'Oréal among the renewals. In 2025 WPP lost roughly **$6.98bn** of account value. In 2026 it lost **$2.81bn** and won more than **$3bn**. The account ledger turned positive.

**New business led the industry.** WPP Media topped the COMvergence H1 2026 rankings with **$5.2bn** of combined new and retained billings — around $600m ahead of its nearest competitor, with retentions making up nearly 40% of the total.

**Wins kept coming.** Kenvue for global creative and production. Reckitt across 21 European markets. Henkel. Haleon. Norwegian Cruise Line. And in May, the one that mattered most: **Jaguar Land Rover**, a global integrated creative and media partnership — structured with **outcome-based remuneration**.

**Margin held while revenue fell.** First-half headline operating margin was 8.4%, **up 0.2 points** like-for-like, on revenue down 4.7%. Full-year margin guidance of 12–13% against 13.0% in 2025. The two-point annual slide had flattened.

**And people got more productive.** This one WPP does not publish, but it can be derived. Revenue less pass-through costs per employee rose roughly **3.2%** year on year — about £47,400 to £48,900 per half. Headcount fell faster (−8.4%) than revenue (−5.6%), and the gap is the gain. WPP was producing more value per person than a year earlier.

That is the clearest public evidence that the AI investment is doing something real — not just cutting cost, but changing output per person.

---

## Chapter Eight — The return
### September to October 2026

**On 1 October 2026**, WPP re-entered the FTSE 100. Ten months after being demoted.

Be precise about what that is and is not. The share price had recovered more than 30%, and market capitalisation was back to around £4.1bn from a trough near £3.1bn. But the places opened up partly because other companies — Beazley and Schroders — were taken over. It is a recovery, not a vindication. WPP is still worth roughly a sixth of its 2017 peak.

Days later, on **4 October**, came a reminder that the transition is not finished. **Jon Cook** — global CEO of VML and CEO of WPP Creative, thirty years in the business — announced he would leave in March 2027. Eric Campbell, a twenty-five-year VML veteran, succeeds him.

March 2027 is the same quarter Elevate28 promises a return to organic growth.

**Next up: the Q3 trading update on 29 October 2026.** It is the first real test of whether the Q1-to-Q2 turn was a trend or a quarter.

# Part Two: What it means

The story above is what happened. This is what we think it signifies.

## 1. The category is changing underneath them

WPP's troubles are often told as a company story. They are also a category story, and separating the two matters for the sprint.

For a century, agencies sold a bundle: strategic judgement, creative ability, production capacity, and media buying leverage. Clients could not easily make the work themselves, so they paid for people who could, and the industry priced that access in **time** — retainers scaled to headcount, project fees estimated from hours.

Generative AI has unbundled that. The ability to produce competent marketing content is now available to anyone with a browser. Not better than WPP's — but good enough, instantly, at near-zero marginal cost.

This is why the holding company model broke, and it is why cost-cutting alone could never have fixed it. WPP's problem was never only that it was too complex. It was that **the thing it sold by the hour was becoming something clients could get for free.**

Every holding company faces this. WPP's distinction is not that it is affected; it is that it fell furthest, fastest, from the greatest height, and so has been forced to respond first and most radically.

## 2. The repositioning: from holding company to operating company

The deepest change in the last eighteen months is not financial. It is a change in **what kind of company WPP is trying to be.**

| | The old WPP | The WPP being built |
|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | Holding company of competing agency brands | Single operating company, four divisions |
| **Sold** | Access to talent, by the hour | Integrated outcomes, increasingly by result |
| **Organised around** | Agency P&Ls | Client needs and regions |
| **Differentiated by** | Creative reputation per brand | Data, integration, governance at scale |
| **Measured by** | Billings and headcount | Margin, outcomes, revenue per person |

Rose has been unusually explicit that this is an identity change rather than a reorganisation. *"A little less holdco and a little more 'co.'"* *"We don't want to be a holding company any more."* *"What's made us successful in the past will not make us successful in the future."*

For a company that is a genuine point of British commercial pride, that is a significant thing to say out loud. The agency brands WPP spent thirty years acquiring are being consolidated. The structure that made it the largest in the world is being dismantled, deliberately, by its own chief executive.

**This is the repositioning: WPP is attempting to stop being a collection of agencies and become a single technology-enabled marketing company.** Everything else — Elevate28, Open, the cost programme, the pricing experiments — is instrumentation for that one move.

## 3. The Microsoft lens

The board's choice of CEO looks less surprising once you read it as a statement about the destination rather than the journey.

Jansen's own words name what they were buying: someone who had supported *"the digital transformation of large enterprises around the world — including embracing AI to create new customer experiences, business models and revenue streams,"* with a record across *"both enterprise and consumer environments."*

Rose spent nine years at Microsoft, finishing as COO of Global Enterprise and, before that, running Western Europe and the UK. That gives her direct operating experience of something WPP has never done, and which we think is the single most important thing she brings:

> **Microsoft serves its largest enterprise customers with dedicated, high-touch account teams, and serves millions of small and mid-sized customers through self-serve product and partners — at the same time, from the same platform, without the second group diluting the first.**

That is a **two-speed commercial model**, and Rose has personally run both speeds.

Look at what WPP has built since she arrived and the shape is familiar:

- A platform that the whole organisation runs on (Open, 75,000 users, >90% of client-facing staff)
- A self-serve tier sold directly to smaller buyers (Open Pro, October 2025)
- A hyperscaler partnership supplying the model layer ($400m to Google, five years)
- A high-touch tier with named global client leaders and outcome-based pay for the largest accounts
- An "Enterprise Solutions" division — language borrowed straight from enterprise software

This is not an advertising turnaround. **It is a software company's go-to-market model, being applied to a services business.** Whether it works is an open question. But it explains the appointment, and it predicts where the company is heading more reliably than anything in the financial statements.

*This reading is ours — an inference from the board's stated rationale, Rose's background, and the shape of what has been built. It is not something WPP has said.*

## 4. The three priorities, decoded

The stated priorities are: satisfy customers, simplify the business, and make Open a margin contributor. Here is what the eighteen months say about each.

### Satisfy customers → already moving, and the proof is retention

The best available measure of client satisfaction is whether clients stay. That number went from **16% to 43%**. Renewals included Huawei, Tesco and L'Oréal.

And WPP Production — the division closest to Open's measurable delivery benefits — is the **only growing division** at +1.6%. It is also the smallest, at 5% of revenue.

> **The read:** WPP's fastest-growing capability is its smallest, and it is the one where the AI investment demonstrably works. This is a scaling problem, not a proof problem. The sprint should stop trying to establish that Open delivers value and start asking why something working at 5% of the business has not propagated to the other 95%.

### Simplify the business → validated internally and externally

Rose named complexity as the root cause. Elevate28 attacks it structurally: one company, four divisions, £500m out.

But the strongest validation came from outside. **The UK Government bought simplification** — 33 suppliers down to 23, faster planning, lower management cost — and paid roughly £1.5bn over four years for it.

> **The read:** simplification is not just an efficiency programme. It is a proposition clients are purchasing, and WPP has now proven it can win on that basis at the largest scale available in the UK market. That is a repeatable sales argument, and it is currently being treated mainly as an internal change programme.

This is also where WPP's challenge and Plansom's philosophy meet. **Simply done** is not a smaller version of the work. It is the removal of everything between the intention and the result — which is precisely what the UK Government paid for, and precisely what Rose says WPP lost.

### Open as a margin contributor → the unresolved one

This is where the story is genuinely unfinished, and it deserves its own section.

## 5. The unresolved tension — and Rose has already named it

Every published WPP Open benefit is expressed as **time saved**. Fourteen hours a week. Four weeks to three hours. Thirty-three times the output from four people in two days. Up to 70% faster production.

Most of WPP's revenue is billed by time.

**Under a time-based model, every hour Open saves is revenue WPP no longer earns.** The better the platform gets, the faster the fee base shrinks. The efficiency goes to the client, and WPP's reward for building the thing is a smaller invoice.

This is not our theory. **Rose has said it herself.** Her stated reasoning is that the time-and-materials model is probably not sustainable long term, precisely because AI lets the work be done faster with fewer people.

So she has the right diagnosis. The difficulty is the timetable.

Asked about moving to outcome-based pay, **Rose has said it will "take a few years."** She has described **Jaguar Land Rover as so far "unique"** in embracing the approach. In the meantime, WPP will run what she calls a **"mixed economy of business models."**

> **This is the gap the sprint should live in.**
>
> The platform is deployed now. The productivity gains are landing now. The pricing model that lets WPP *keep* those gains is, by the CEO's own account, years away and currently proven on one account.
>
> For the period in between — which is the period we are in — WPP is getting better at doing work under contracts that pay it less for being better.

There are only three ways out, and all three are live at WPP:

1. **Outcome pricing** — get paid for results. JLR is the template. Rose says years.
2. **Volume expansion** — the same fee buys 33× the assets, and clients buy more because the unit economics changed. Hawkstone gestures at it; nothing proves it at scale.
3. **New revenue entirely** — reach buyers who were never going to hire an agency at all.

**The third is the fastest, and it is the one Open Pro was built for.** That is where the hypothesis goes.

## 6. Where the moat actually is

One more thing the story establishes, because it determines how Open can be sold without damaging the brand.

Open Pro's generation runs on other people's models — Google's Imagen and Veo, OpenAI's DALL-E and Sora, Adobe Firefly, with text from Gemini, Claude and ChatGPT. WPP has committed $400m to Google over five years for the privilege. Meanwhile Google and Canva sell competing marketing tools of their own.

**So the generation capability cannot be the moat.** It is rented, and it is available to everyone — including WPP's own clients, directly, today.

If WPP's promise were "we make content," that promise is already commoditised, and the dilution happened before Open Pro existed.

What WPP has that the model providers do not:

- Proprietary audience and brand data at global scale
- Brand governance and safety — the machinery that stops a brand publishing something that damages it
- Integration across media, creative and production in one place
- Accountability for a commercial outcome, with a balance sheet behind it
- Thirty years of institutional knowledge about what actually works for large brands

> **The brand question answers itself once you see this.** Open Pro does not dilute WPP by reaching smaller clients. It would only dilute WPP if it were sold as *cheap creative*. Sold as **governed, brand-safe execution**, it extends the brand into territory the brand could never previously reach.

# Part Three: The hypothesis

Everything above points at one strategic idea. We think it is the right one to test, and we think the AI Impact Sprint is the way to test it.

## The hypothesis

> **WPP's next phase of growth comes from the long tail, not the top.**
>
> **Concentrate WPP's best people on the top 100 clients** — where half the revenue sits, where relationships are the product, and where simplicity, seniority and accountability win and keep the work.
>
> **Serve everyone else through the platform.** There is a very large population of smaller businesses that want the WPP factor — the judgement, the craft, the brand discipline — and have never been able to afford the fee. WPP Open makes that economically possible for the first time.
>
> **And position it on control, not creativity.** Every general-purpose AI tool already offers creativity, speed and infinite options. None offers **brand control**: one place where the brand's rules, data, assets, history and agents live together, and where everything produced is on-brand, on-strategy and safe to publish.
>
> **That is the sweet spot — and nobody else can occupy it.**

## Why the evidence supports it

| Evidence from the eighteen months | What it supports |
|---|---|
| Top 10 clients = 21% of revenue; top 100 ≈ 50% | Concentration is real. A top-ten client is worth roughly **67×** a tail client. These relationships justify the best people |
| ~1,600 clients outside the top 100 average **≤£3.2m**, on 90-day contracts | A large, under-served tail already inside the building |
| Open Pro built explicitly for those who *"cannot afford a full-service integrated agency"* | WPP has already built the product for this segment |
| Open Pro runs on rented, commodity models | Generation cannot be the differentiator — control must be |
| UK Government bought **simplicity**, not creativity | Buyers are purchasing clarity and control. Demand is demonstrated |
| Production — the most platform-enabled division — is the only one growing | The delivery model works where it has been applied |
| Rose's Microsoft background: enterprise high-touch **and** self-serve scale | The CEO has personally run a two-speed model |
| Revenue per head up **3.2%** | The platform genuinely changes the economics of delivery |
| Outcome pricing *"will take a few years"* | New revenue is the faster of the three escapes from the time-billing trap |

## What must be true for it to work

Stated as tests rather than assertions, because this is a hypothesis and should be able to fail.

| # | What must be true | How we would know |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Open Pro revenue is **incremental**, not substitutional — it reaches buyers who were never going to retain an agency | Client-level revenue history before and after adoption. If existing tail clients downgrade from managed services to Open Pro, the hypothesis inverts into a cannibalisation risk |
| **2** | **Brand control is something buyers will pay for** — enough to choose WPP over a free or cheap general-purpose tool | Win/loss analysis against Canva, Google and in-house. What do buyers say the deciding factor was? |
| **3** | **Cost-to-serve is low enough** to make tail revenue profitable at volume | Onboarding and support cost per Open Pro account, against revenue per account |
| **4** | Concentrating senior people on the top 100 **improves** those relationships rather than merely redistributing scarcity | Retention and revenue trajectory in the top 100 specifically, not group-wide |
| **5** | The tail experience still feels like **WPP** | Does Open Pro deliver a recognisably WPP-quality result without a WPP team? If not, the brand thins as the product scales |
| **6** | The segmentation line **holds commercially** — Open Pro and managed services do not compete for the same buyer | Does the sales organisation have a clear, enforced rule? Today it is not written down |

**Tests 1 and 2 are the critical path.** Test 1 decides whether this is growth or internal transfer. Test 2 decides whether the positioning is defensible. The rest are important but secondary.

## What the sprint would do

The sprint's job is not to prove WPP Open works. That is established — 75,000 users, rising revenue per head, the only growing division.

**The sprint's job is to establish whether WPP can keep the value Open creates, and whether the long tail is where that value can be kept.**

Specifically:

1. **Answer test 1 from existing data.** WPP already holds the billing history. This is an analysis, not a research project, and it can be done in days.
2. **Pressure-test the control positioning** against how buyers actually choose.
3. **Model the tail economics** — revenue per account against cost-to-serve, at the volumes that would make it material.
4. **Draft the segmentation rule** that keeps Open Pro and managed services out of each other's way.
5. **Identify what to simplify first** — because a tail business only works if onboarding, pricing and support are radically simpler than anything WPP does today.

That last point is where this becomes a Plansom problem rather than only a WPP one. A two-speed model fails on operational complexity long before it fails on strategy. **Serving 1,600 small clients profitably is not a smaller version of serving 100 large ones — it is a different discipline, and the discipline is simplicity.**

## The one-line version

> **WPP spent thirty years getting big by buying agencies. It will spend the next three getting valuable by removing them — concentrating its best people on the relationships that justify them, and reaching everyone else through a platform that sells the one thing no general-purpose AI can offer: control.**
